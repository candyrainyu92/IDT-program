Glossary of Key Terminologies in the Field of IDT

Terms Our Definition Why it matters
1. AECT
(Association for Educational Communications and Technology)		 AECT is an international professional organization that supports the development of Instructional Design and Technology through research, professional definitions, academic publications, and ethical standards. It provides professional guidelines that help instructional designers to improve educational practices. AECT shapes the field’s professional identity and practices and helps guide the advancement of Instructional Design and Technology through research, resources, professional standards, and ethical practices.
2. The ADDIE model
(Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation and Evaluation)		 The ADDIE model acts as a conceptual representation and systematic workflow comprising Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, and Evaluation. It guides instructional designers through identifying learning needs, planning instructional strategies, developing learning materials, implementing instruction, and evaluating its effectiveness. These activities can be revisited as needed. It provides a flexible framework that guides instructional designers through the entire design process, ensuring that learning needs, objectives, instructional strategies, and assessments are aligned to create effective learning solutions.
3. Behaviorism Behaviorism is a learning theory that focuses on observable changes in learners’ behavior resulting from practice, repetition, reinforcement, and feedback. It is useful when learners need to develop specific foundational skills through clearly stated objectives and measurable performance. For example, beginning Spanish students may practice vocabulary, pronunciation, or basic sentence structures and receive immediate feedback to reinforce correct responses.
4. Bloom’s Taxonomy Bloom’s Taxonomy is a hierarchical framework that classifies cognitive learning objectives into six progressive levels, from less complex to more complex cognitive processes: remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, and create. It helps instructional designers develop learning outcomes and objectives at different levels, such as course, modules, lessons, and activities, while ensuring alignment between different levels and assessment.
5. B. F. Skinner B. F. Skinner was a psychologist and an influential scholar in behavioral learning theory. He is known for operant conditioning, which explains how the consequences of a behavior influence whether that behavior is likely to happen again. One of the most influential scholars advocated behaviorism learning theory to help instructional designers to understand the theory of behavioral learning and be able to apply it to design the projects with measurable learning objectives, structured practice, immediate feedback, reinforcement.
6. Cognitive Load Theory Cognitive Load Theory explains how the limited capacity of working memory affects learning when learners process new information. It guides designers to minimize unnecessary materials, reduce cognitive demands, manage the complexity of information, and support learners organizing new knowledge effectively in the learning process. Cognitive Load Theory is important because it helps instructional designers organize information clearly, reduce unnecessary mental effort, and make learning effective for learners.
7. Connectivism Connectivism is a learning perspective that explains learning as the process of creating connections among people, digital resources, information, and communities. It emphasizes the ability to find, evaluate, connect, and update knowledge in the rapidly changing digital environment. Connectivism helps instructional designers create learning activities that encourage learners to build connections and learn through networks of people and digital resources such as databases, online communities, and Artificial Intelligence tools.
8. Constructivism Constructivism is a learning theory in which learners actively build understanding by connecting new information with their prior knowledge in real-world contexts. Instruction based on constructivism allows students to explore, collaborate, solve authentic problems, and create products that demonstrate their own understanding. It provides a theoretical foundation for instructional designers to design learner-centered activities, authentic learning environments and appropriate scaffolding that support active knowledge construction.
9. Dick & Carey Model
(Systems Approach Model)		 The Dick and Carey Model is a systematic instructional design model introduced by Walter Dick and Lou Carey in 1978. It is also known as the Systems Approach Model. It views instruction as a system of interconnected components, so changes in one component may affect the others. Its ten main components are: identify instructional goals; conduct instructional analysis; analyze learners and contexts; write performance objectives; develop assessment instruments; develop instructional strategies; develop and select instructional materials; design and conduct formative evaluation; revise instruction; and design and conduct summative evaluation. It is a high alignment design model easy for the beginner of instructional designers to follow the clear, structured roadmap to design the project.
10. E-Learning E-learning is a learning approach that uses digital technologies like networks, online platforms to develop instructional content, facilitate learning activities, and support assessment and interaction in synchronous or asynchronous. E-learning enables instructional designers to create flexible, accessible, and scalable learning experiences, allowing learners to engage in interactive activities and learn across different locations and schedules.
11. Formative Assessment Formative Assessment is an ongoing process of gathering and using evidence of learners’ understanding and progress during learning, identifying learning gaps and adjusting instructional strategies to improve learning outcomes. Formative Assessment is important because it helps instructional designers identify learners' learning gaps, provide feedback, and improve instruction during the learning process.
12. Formative Evaluation Formative evaluation is the ongoing data collection and analysis of evidence during the development or implementation of instruction to identify strengths, weaknesses, and what needs improvement. Guiding revisions to enhance instructional effectiveness. It helps instructional designers identify design problems early, gather feedback from learners, and stakeholder, and make evidence-based revisions before an instructional product, lesson, or program is fully complete.
13. Gagné’s Nine Events of Instruction Robert M. Gagné organizes learning into nine sequence instruction events to support instructional designers to design projects. The nine events are: gain attention; inform learners of the objectives; stimulate recall of prior learning; present the content; provide learning guidance; elicit performance through practice; provide feedback; assess performance; and enhance retention and transfer so learners can remember and apply what they have learned in new situations. Gagné’s Nine Events of Instruction plays an important role for learners' effective learning through step-by-step process. It provides instructional designers with a structure for organizing learning activities, guiding learners through the learning process, and aligning instruction with learning objectives.
14. Instructional design and technology (IDT) IDT is an ethical field that applies learning theories, systematic design processes, and technology to analyze learner’s needs, design and develop learning experiences that address instructional or non-instructional problems to improve learning and performance. IDT is important for professionals to apply learning theories, strategies, design models, instructional design practices, and technology to improve learners’ learning experience and performance.
15. Learning Experience Design (LXD) LXD is a learner-centered approach that combines instructional design, learning sciences, and user experience design to create meaningful, engaging, and effective learning journeys. LXD places the learners’ experience at the center of the design process. Effective LXD catches the learners’ attention and enhances their experiences, engaging, and accessibility, ultimately supporting better learning outcomes.
16. Needs Assessment Needs Assessment is a systematic process of identifying gaps between current and desired performance, investigating their underlying causes, and determining whether instructional or non-instructional solutions are needed. Needs Assessment helps instructional designers recognize the root causes of performance gaps and design instructional or non-instructional solutions that ensure design decisions are evidence-based and address actual needs.
17. Non-instructional solution A non-instructional solution is an intervention used when a performance problem is not primarily caused by a lack of knowledge or skills and therefore cannot be solved through instruction design alone. Non-instructional solutions help instructional designers address the causes of performance gap rather than assuming every problem requires training. These solutions may involve improving the broader environment, resources, procedures, expectations, incentives, communication to support effective performance.
18. SAM (Successive Approximation Model) SAM is an iterative instructional design model that emphasizes rapid prototyping, continuous feedback, and ongoing revisions rather than following a strictly linear process. SAM is widely used in professional instructional design because it enables designers to rapidly develop prototypes, gather feedback, and refine learning solutions efficiently.
19. Scaffolding Scaffolding is an instructional strategy that helps learners complete tasks they cannot yet perform independently. The support is adjusted to learners’ needs and gradually removed as their competence develops. It helps instructional designers create learning experiences that match learners' needs and current abilities, manage task complexity, and gradually guide learners toward independent learning.
20. Self-assessment It is a learner-centered assessment strategy in which learners evaluate their own knowledge, skills, and progress against established learning objectives, rubric, or criteria. It is widely used in instructional design, particularly in formative assessment and self-paced learning. It helps learners monitor their progress, identify learning gaps, and make inform-decisions about next steps.
21. Summative Evaluation It’s an evaluation conducted at the end of an instructional program or intervention to determine its overall effectiveness and whether intended outcomes were achieved. It provides evidence for judging the success of instruction and making decisions about continuing revising or expanding a program.
